Search for anything about quoting in custom manufacturing and you will find two kinds of pages: instant-quote platforms telling buyers to upload a CAD file, and RFQ checklists telling buyers what to include. Almost nothing is written for the people on the other side of that email: the molder, the fabricator, the machine shop, the sign maker, the manufacturer whose product is configured rather than picked from a catalog, and whose quoting desk is quietly deciding how fast the whole company grows. This post is for that side.

## Why configured quoting is slow, precisely

A catalog product has a price. A configured product has a calculation, and the calculation needs judgment: geometry, material at today's cost, routing, tooling, tolerances, quantity breaks. In most shops that judgment lives in one or two senior people who are also needed on the floor, so RFQs wait. The published turnaround norm in custom work runs three to five business days, and the same body of data carries the uncomfortable half: quotes returned within two hours have been reported winning over 90% of the time, while quotes after five days win under 5%. Whatever your win rate is, your response time is quietly setting its ceiling.

The delay has one root cause in shop after shop: every quote is assembled from scratch, even when the shop has quoted something nearly identical before. The knowledge exists. It lives in folders, old spreadsheets, and the estimator's memory, none of which scale.

## The four flavors of the problem

**Optioned assemblies.** Products built from a base plus selections: frames, panels, enclosures, cab systems. The quote is a configuration walk, and the errors come from missed option interactions. The fix shape is a structured intake that captures the configuration once, plus retrieval of the closest priced configurations from your own history.

**Tolerance-driven machined parts.** Here geometry is the estimate: a tighter callout changes the routing. The estimator cannot be replaced, but the archaeology around them can be, pulling the three nearest parts you have actually run, with the hours they actually took. We published [a Kokomo shop's quoting numbers, worked in full](/research/sample-engagement-indiana-machine-shop-quoting), including the arithmetic of what the six-day median was costing.

**Catalog-plus-custom hybrids.** A 15,000-part catalog where half the orders are variants. The trap is treating variant quotes like catalog lookups; the fix is knowing instantly which side of the line an inquiry falls on, and drafting the variant quote from the nearest catalog ancestor.

**Configurator-fed orders.** The website configurator produces a spec; a human still turns it into a price by hand. The configuration is already structured data, which makes this the easiest flavor to fix and the most commonly neglected. ## What faster actually requires

Not a platform, and not replacing the estimator. Three capabilities, in order: structured intake, so an emailed RFQ with a PDF becomes fields instead of retyping; retrieval, so your own quote and job history is searchable by similarity instead of by memory; and drafting, so the estimator opens a prepared quote and applies judgment to the twenty percent that is genuinely new. Every published result we have seen for this pattern lands in the same band, 30 to 50% less time per quote, and the elapsed-time collapse matters more than the hours: same-day responses on repeat-class work is what moves win rates. ## Frequently asked questions

### How long should a custom manufacturing quote take?

Published norms run three to five business days, but the win-rate data is blunt: two-hour responses have been reported winning over 90% while five-day responses win under 5%. The practical target for repeat-class work is same day; for genuinely new work, whatever your engineering honestly requires, communicated fast.

### Can AI quote custom parts without an estimator?

Not credibly, and be wary of anyone who says otherwise. What holds up in practice is AI handling intake, history retrieval, and the first draft, with the estimator making every judgment call and owning every number that goes out.
